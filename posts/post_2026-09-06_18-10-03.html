TITLE: The Bennington Triangle: The Forgotten Mountain That Swallowed Five People Without a Trace

Deep within the dense, sun-starved wilderness of southwestern Vermont lies Glastenbury Mountain—a majestic peak with a spine-chilling reputation. To the casual hiker, it appears to be an idyllic stretch of the Appalachian Trail. But locals and seasoned folklorists know the truth: this region, dubbed the Bennington Triangle, holds one of the darkest, most inexplicable mysteries in American history.

Between 1945 and 1950, five people vanished off the face of the earth inside this dense forest. No footprints. No clothing. No remains. Despite massive FBI manhunts and hundreds of searchers combing every square inch of the woods, the mountain seemed to simply open up and swallow its victims whole. What happened during those five cursed years, and why did an entire town pack up and abandon the mountain forever?

The Hunter Who Knew the Woods Too Well (1945)

The nightmare began on November 12, 1945. Middie Rivers, a 74-year-old experienced mountain guide, was leading a group of four hunters down the Long Trail near Glastenbury Mountain. Rivers knew every ridge, valley, and stream in the region—he had navigated these woods for decades.

As the party neared an area known as Bickford Hollow, Rivers walked slightly ahead of the group. He turned a bend in the trail, stepping out of sight for less than a minute. When his companions rounded the corner, Rivers was gone. Thinking he had played a joke, they shouted his name into the quiet forest, but only silence answered.

An extensive search involving local police, volunteers, and the U.S. Army combed the woods for weeks. The only clue ever recovered was a single rifle cartridge found in a nearby creek bed, which companions confirmed belonged to Rivers. It was as if the veteran woodsman had evaporated into thin air.

The Girl in the Red Coat Who Never Came Home (1946)

Just over a year later, on December 1, 1946, the mountain claimed its most famous victim. Paula Welden, an 18-year-old sophomore at Bennington College, decided to spend her Sunday afternoon hiking a section of the Long Trail. She wore a bright red parka—a detail that would haunt searchers for years.

Several witnesses saw Paula on the trail that day. An elderly couple even spoke with her, warning her that night was approaching and she wasn't dressed warmly enough for the dropping temperatures. Paula laughed off the warning and continued up the trail toward Glastenbury Mountain.

She was never seen again.

When Paula failed to return to her dorm, one of the largest missing-person searches in New England history was launched. FBI agents, state troopers, bloodhounds, and military helicopters scoured the wilderness. Her wealthy family offered a massive reward. Yet, despite the bright red coat she was wearing against the gray forest background, not a single thread was ever found. Her disappearance spooked the state of Vermont so deeply that it led directly to the creation of the Vermont State Police.

The Man Who Vanished From a Moving Bus (1949)

Three years to the day after Paula Welden vanished, the Bennington Triangle delivered its most terrifying, mathematically impossible anomaly.

On December 1, 1949, James E. Tetford, a military veteran, boarded a bus in St. Albans, Vermont, returning to his home at the Soldiers' Home in Bennington. Other passengers remembered seeing Tetford sitting in his seat, sleeping soundly as the bus made its final leg toward Bennington.

When the bus arrived at its destination, Tetford was gone. His luggage remained in the overhead rack. His bus schedule sat open on his empty seat. The bus had made no stops between the last station and Bennington, and the driver swore no one had stepped off the vehicle. Tetford had literally vanished from inside a enclosed, moving bus while traveling through the shadow of Glastenbury Mountain.

The Boy and the Experienced Hiker (1950)

The terror peaked in the autumn of 1950 with two back-to-back disappearances in a single month.

On October 12, 1950, 8-year-old Paul Jepson was playing in the family truck while his mother tended to pigs on a local farm near Glastenbury. She stepped away for no more than five minutes. When she returned, Paul was missing. Bloodhounds brought to the scene picked up the boy’s scent and tracked it directly to the middle of a nearby highway—where the trail vanished instantly, as if he had been lifted into the sky.

Sixteen days later, on October 28, 38-year-old Frieda Langer, an experienced hiker who knew the terrain well, was trekking with her cousin near the Somerset Reservoir. After slipping into a cold mountain stream, she told her cousin she was running back to their campsite to change her clothes. The campsite was less than half a mile away.

She never made it. Searchers conducted 19 separate operations using over 300 men, aircraft, and radio equipment. Nothing was found. Then, seven months later, in May 1951, Frieda's body suddenly reappeared in an open field that had been searched dozens of times before. Her body was so badly decomposed that coroners could not determine a cause of death. She remains the only victim of the Bennington Triangle whose body was ever recovered.

Curse, Portal, or Serial Killer?

Long before white settlers arrived in Vermont, local Native American tribes refused to tread on Glastenbury Mountain. They believed the land was cursed, claiming it was home to a "man-eating wind" that swept down the peak and swallowed anyone who lingered past dusk. Tribal legends spoke of an enchanted stone that opened up to swallow travelers whole.

Modern researchers have offered various theories over the decades:

  • Unmapped Abandonded Mineshafts: Glastenbury was once a logging and mining hub. Some believe victims fell down hidden vertical shafts lost to overgrown vegetation.
  • A Regional Serial Killer: The proximity in dates—specifically the December 1st timeline—led many to suspect a predator who knew the dense woods inside and out.
  • Paranormal Anomaly: UFO sightings, strange lights, anomalous acoustic phenomena, and localized magnetic shifts have all been recorded around the mountain for over a century.

The Ghost Town Left Behind

Today, the town of Glastenbury is virtually dead. Driven away by economic decline and choked by the dark history hanging over the woods, residents abandoned the settlement. In 1937, the state of Vermont officially revoked the town's charter. Today, its population hovers near zero.

The trees have reclaimed the roads, the houses have decayed into moss, and the wilderness of the Bennington Triangle remains quiet, cold, and watching. If you ever find yourself walking the Long Trail as afternoon turns to twilight, keep your eyes on the path—and whatever you do, don't wander off alone.
